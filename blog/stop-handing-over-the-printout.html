Stop handing over the printout

Presentation · July 24, 2026 · 5 min read

Watch a coordinator who is not confident yet, and you will see the same move: the printout comes out early and gets slid across the desk.

It is a comfort object. Paper gives you something to point at, something to hide behind, and a way to deliver bad news without saying it out loud. It also ends your presentation the moment it hits the desk.

What happens when a parent gets the paper

They stop listening to you and start reading. Not the whole thing — they scan for the largest number, find it, and everything you say from that point is heard through it.

Worse, treatment plan printouts are designed by practice management software for internal use. They list procedure codes. They itemise per tooth. They frequently show your full fee next to an insurance estimate in a way that looks, to an untrained reader, like you are charging twice.

You did not present a plan. You handed over a document optimised for a billing department and asked a parent to interpret it.

Ask someone outside dentistry to read one of your treatment plan printouts and tell you what it says. The answer is usually sobering, and it is the fastest way to see the problem yourself.

The order that works

Screen first, paper last.

Present from the monitor — the photo, the x-ray, your finger on the tooth. The parent's attention stays in the same place as yours. You control the pace. You reach the number when you have decided to, not when they find it.

Then, at the end, the printout goes with them as a record of what you discussed. Not as the vehicle for discussing it.

When you do hand it over, tell them what it is:

“This is just a record of what we went through, and the codes are for your insurance more than for you. The number that matters is this one here — that is your part.”

Circle it. Actually circle it, with a pen. A parent who leaves with one circled figure on a page is in a completely different position from one holding four pages of undifferentiated line items.

What to give them instead

The most useful thing a parent can leave with is not the full plan. It is a small number of specific things they can repeat to a partner:

  • What is wrong, in one sentence
  • What we are doing first, and why that one
  • The out-of-pocket figure, or the monthly
  • When it is scheduled

Four things. Written down. That is a case that survives the drive home.

The exception

Some parents are readers. They will ask for the document early and they genuinely want to see the itemisation before they hear anything else. Usually the analytically-minded ones, and often the ones with the most questions.

Give it to them. Fighting a parent for control of a piece of paper is not a winnable exchange, and refusing looks evasive.

But go through it with them rather than watching them read it. “Let me walk you through what you are looking at, because these codes are not obvious.” You keep the narration, which is the part that matters.

The underlying point

The printout is a record of a decision. It is not the mechanism for making one.

Every practice has a coordinator whose acceptance rate is noticeably higher than everyone else's, and if you watch them closely, one of the things you will notice is that the paper comes out at the end. Not because someone taught them a rule. Because they worked out that a conversation beats a document, and the document was interrupting the conversation.
