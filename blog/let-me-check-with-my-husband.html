“Let me check with my husband”

Objections · By Nick Fairbanks · July 2, 2026 · 5 min read

This one is usually honest. That is what makes it dangerous.

Unlike most stalls, “let me check with my husband” is generally a real constraint (and it works the same way when it is a wife or a partner). Two people share the money, and one of them is standing in your office while the other is at work. Nobody is inventing an excuse.

The problem is what happens next. The parent leaves, and somewhere around eight that evening the conversation gets replayed at a kitchen table by someone who is not you, from memory, at the end of a long day, to a partner who did not see the x-ray.

That is where the case dies. Not in your office.

Why the retelling goes badly

Put yourself in the parent's position. They spent maybe twenty minutes with you. They saw an image they could not fully read. They heard a number. Now they have to defend a decision they are not certain about, to someone who was not there, and who is going to ask the obvious question.

“Do we have to do all of it right now?”

And the parent does not know. Because you covered it, but you covered it in the middle of six other things, and it did not stick.

So they say the safest possible thing: “Let's think about it.” Which is how a case that was fully accepted in the room becomes a voicemail you leave twice next week.

The mistake almost everyone makes here is treating this as an objection to overcome. It is not. It is a logistics problem. You are not trying to change their mind. You are trying to make sure the second conversation goes as well as the first one did.

Three ways to survive the kitchen table

Get the partner on the phone now

The most direct option and the most underused. Not because it does not work — because coordinators feel awkward suggesting it.

“Would it help if we just called him? It takes two minutes and then he can ask me directly instead of you having to remember all of it.”

Notice the framing. You are not pushing for a decision. You are removing a burden from the parent in front of you, which is exactly how it lands. Plenty of them will take you up on it, and a phone call where the doctor's recommendation comes from you is worth more than any printout.

Arm them for the question you know is coming

If the call is not going to happen, then your job for the next ninety seconds is to prepare the parent to answer one specific question.

“When you talk tonight, he is probably going to ask whether it all has to happen at once. So — it does not. The one we need to handle in the next few weeks is the lower right molar, because that one is close to the nerve. The rest can wait until after the new year.”

Now the parent has an answer. A specific one, with a reason attached. That is a completely different conversation from “I think they said she needs a bunch of work done.”

Write down the number they will actually be asked about

Not the treatment plan total. The monthly figure, or the out-of-pocket for phase one. Whatever the real question is going to be.

Write it on something. Physically. A parent holding a card with “$186/mo, starts after insurance” on it is in a much better position than one scrolling through a six-page printout looking for the part that matters.

What not to do

  • Do not offer to call him yourself, later, without her. Calling together, right now, keeps her in the conversation. Calling him separately goes around the person in front of you, and it makes her feel like an obstacle rather than a parent.
  • Do not say “of course, take your time” and end there. That is not respect, it is abdication. You can be entirely respectful and still spend two more minutes making sure they can explain it.
  • Do not schedule the follow-up call for a week out. Make it two days. The details fade fast, and the longer the gap the more the whole thing recedes into something that felt expensive and can probably wait.

The tell

Here is the useful part. There is a version of this objection that is genuine, and a version that is cover for something else, and you can usually distinguish them with one question.

“Of course. Is there anything you think he is going to push back on, so I can make sure you have the answer?”

If it is genuine, you get a real answer. “He is going to say we just paid for my son's braces.” Now you know it is budget, and you can work with that.

If the parent goes vague — “no, I just need to talk to him” — the spouse may not be the actual issue. Something else did not land, and the husband is the exit. That is worth knowing before they walk out.

Either way you learned something. And it took one sentence.
