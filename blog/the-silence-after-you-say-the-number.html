The silence after you say the number

Objections · July 14, 2026 · 5 min read

You say the number. The parent does not say anything. And in that gap, most coordinators start talking again — and give away the case.

It goes like this. “The total is three thousand four hundred.” Pause. The parent's face does something unreadable. And before they have opened their mouth, out it comes: “—but we do have payment plans, and we could always start with just the front two, and honestly a lot of families spread this out, and if that is still too much we could look at—”

Nobody objected. You negotiated against yourself, unprompted, in about six seconds.

Why the silence feels so bad

Because you know what the number means to them. You have some idea what most families in your area earn, and you just asked for a meaningful chunk of it. If you have any empathy at all, that lands as guilt, and the fastest way to relieve guilt is to start offering things.

There is a second reason, less comfortable. Many coordinators are not fully convinced the price is fair. Not because it is not — because nobody has ever walked them through how the practice arrives at its fees, so they are quoting numbers they cannot personally defend. That uncertainty shows up as flinching.

If that is you, go ask. Ask the doctor what goes into the fee for a stainless steel crown. Lab, chair time, materials, the sedation, the staff, the overhead per operatory hour. Not so you can recite it to parents — so that you stop apologising for it.

What the silence actually is

It is arithmetic.

Most of the time the parent is not recoiling. They are doing sums. What is in checking, what is on the card, when payday is, whether this collides with the car thing. That takes a few seconds and it looks like nothing from the outside.

Interrupt it and you do two things. You stop them mid-calculation, so they never reach an answer. And you signal that even you think the number is too high — because why else would you be rushing to reduce it?

A discount offered before it is asked for is not generous. It is an admission. The parent now knows the first number was soft, which means the second one might be too, and the conversation has quietly stopped being about treatment.

The drill

Say the number. Then stop talking until they speak. That is the whole technique and it is much harder than it sounds.

Some specifics that make it survivable:

Say it as a complete sentence with a full stop. Not “it's around thirty-four hundred or so, depending.” Hedging invites a challenge. “The total is three thousand four hundred dollars.” Then nothing.

Keep your face neutral and your body still. Do not wince. Do not slide the paper across apologetically. Parents read your face before they read the number, and if you look uncomfortable they will conclude they should be too.

Count to five in your head if you must. Four seconds feels like thirty in a quiet room. Almost nobody makes it past six before speaking.

Have something to do with your hands. Practical, and it works. Look at the treatment plan rather than at them. It converts the silence from a standoff into a normal pause.

What comes back

You will get one of four responses, and every one is more useful than anything you would have said in the gap.

“Okay.” — More common than coordinators expect. Move to scheduling. Do not celebrate, do not offer a payment plan they did not ask for.

“How much is that per month?” — They have accepted the treatment and are solving for cash flow. Best possible outcome.

“Wow.” — Honest reaction, not a rejection. Acknowledge it once — “it is a real number, I know” — and then be quiet again.

“What does insurance cover?” — They are looking for the number to be smaller. Fine. Now you are in a factual conversation instead of a guessing one.

Every one of those is a door they opened. You do not have to guess which objection you are handling, because they told you.

The uncomfortable part

Some parents will say no. Letting the silence run does not prevent that, and this is not a technique for extracting yes from someone who cannot afford it.

What it prevents is the other failure — the one where a parent who could have said yes gets handed a smaller plan they did not ask for, accepts the reduced version, and leaves with treatment their child still needs.

That case looks like a win on the day. It is not.

Practice the conversation before it counts

Practice saying the number and stopping — the hardest four seconds in the job.
