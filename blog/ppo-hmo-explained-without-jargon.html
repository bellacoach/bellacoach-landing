How to explain PPO and HMO without using the words PPO or HMO

Insurance · By Nick Fairbanks · July 11, 2026 · 6 min read

The fastest way to lose a parent is to answer a question about money with an explanation of insurance architecture.

They asked what it costs. You started talking about in-network fee schedules and annual maximums and whether the plan has a waiting period. Twelve seconds in, they stopped listening and started worrying, because the only signal they got was that this is complicated and complicated usually means expensive.

Almost every coordinator does this early on. It comes from wanting to be accurate. But precision about the mechanism is not the same as clarity about the outcome, and parents only need one of those.

What they are actually asking

When a parent says “what does my insurance cover?”, they are asking one of exactly three things:

  • What do I pay today?
  • Is that the whole amount or will something else arrive later?
  • Am I getting a fair deal here?

None of those require them to understand the difference between plan types. You need to understand it. They need the number and the confidence that it is real.

The plain-language version

If you must explain the structure — and sometimes you must, because they ask directly — here is the version that survives contact with a tired parent.

For a PPO:

“Your plan works like a discount plus a contribution. We have an agreed price with them that is lower than our normal fee, and then they pay a share of that. What is left is yours. There is also a cap on what they will pay in a year — you are nowhere near it, so it does not affect us today.”

For an HMO:

“Your plan works from a fixed price list. For each procedure there is a set amount you pay, and that does not change no matter what our normal fee is. The good news is it is predictable — this is the number, today and in six months.”

One structural fact an HMO family does need: a dental HMO generally only pays when they see the dentist or office they are assigned to. If your office is not theirs, say that before anything else.

No acronyms. No “fee schedule.” No “annual maximum” unless it is actually going to bite, in which case say it plainly: “they stop paying after fifteen hundred a year, and we are going to use about eight hundred of that, so you have room.”

The trap in the word “covered”

This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in the whole conversation.

To you, “covered at 80%” means the plan pays eighty percent of an agreed fee, usually after the deductible. To a parent, “covered” means paid for. When they hear a procedure is covered, a large share of them believe they owe nothing, and they will be genuinely upset when a statement arrives.

So do not use the word alone. Attach the number every single time.

“That one is covered at eighty percent after your deductible. You’ve already met the deductible this year, so of the four hundred, they pay three-twenty and your part is eighty dollars.”

Check whether the deductible has been met before you quote that number. If it has not, the patient pays the deductible first, and a $50 deductible turns that eighty dollars into a hundred and twenty. It takes one extra sentence, and it removes the most common source of angry phone calls in the practice.

If you want one habit out of this whole piece: never say a percentage without immediately saying the dollar amount it produces. Percentages are abstract. Dollars are the thing the parent is actually deciding about.

When the plan is bad

Sometimes coverage is genuinely poor. A plan with a low annual cap, a waiting period, or a missing-tooth clause that rules out the thing you just recommended.

Say it early and say it flatly. Do not bury it and hope the parent works it out from the printout.

“I want to tell you something upfront so it is not a surprise later. Your plan has a twelve-month wait on crowns, and you enrolled in March. So for this one they are not going to contribute anything — the full amount is out of pocket. Let me show you what we can do about that.”

If the answer is to start smaller and phase it, say so early. Parents forgive bad insurance. They do not forgive finding out about it after they said yes. The first is their employer's fault. The second is yours.

The question you should ask before any of this

Before you explain anything, find out what they already believe.

“Before I go through this — has your plan worked the way you expected so far?”

You will occasionally get an entire history in response. A parent who got burned by a surprise bill two years ago is not going to trust your estimate no matter how carefully you build it, and you need to know that before you start rather than after they go quiet.

Their answer tells you whether your job today is explaining, or rebuilding trust that someone else broke.

The terms, for you, not for them

Coordinators need the vocabulary even when parents never should hear it. Here is each term with the plain phrase to use instead.

TermWhat it meansSay this instead
DeductibleThe amount the patient pays each benefit year before the plan starts paying its percentage.“The first [amount] this year is yours, then the plan starts sharing.”
Annual maximumThe most the plan will pay in a benefit year.“They stop paying after [amount] a year.”
Waiting periodTime after enrolling before the plan covers a type of treatment.“Your plan does not start paying for this until [date].”
Missing-tooth clauseThe plan will not pay to replace a tooth lost before coverage began.“Because that tooth was missing before you joined the plan, they will not help with replacing it.”
In-network fee scheduleThe reduced prices the practice agreed with the plan.“We have an agreed price with your plan that is lower than our normal fee.”
Coordination of benefitsHow two plans split a bill when a patient has both.“With two plans, the second one may pick up some of what the first does not.”

Quick answers

What is the difference between a dental PPO and an HMO?

A PPO pays a share of an agreed fee, usually after a deductible and up to an annual maximum, and lets the patient choose their dentist. A dental HMO (DHMO) charges a fixed copay per procedure, but generally only at the dentist or office the patient is assigned to.

What does “covered at 80%” mean?

The plan pays 80% of the agreed fee for that procedure, usually after the deductible, and the patient pays the remaining 20%. It does not mean the patient owes nothing.

What is a dental insurance waiting period?

A stretch of time after someone enrolls, often months, before the plan will pay for certain treatment such as crowns. Treatment done before it ends is out of pocket.
