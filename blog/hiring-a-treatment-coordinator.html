What to look for when hiring a treatment coordinator

The role · By Nick Fairbanks · August 3, 2026 · 6 min read

Most practices hire a treatment coordinator by promoting the friendliest person at the front desk. Sometimes that works. Often it does not, and nobody can articulate why.

The reason is that the two jobs share almost no skills. Front desk rewards efficiency, warmth, and never letting the schedule slip. Treatment coordination rewards sitting in an uncomfortable conversation without rushing it. Those pull in opposite directions.

What does not predict success

Years in dentistry. Useful for vocabulary, nearly irrelevant to case acceptance. Some of the best coordinators come from outside dentistry entirely. Clinical terms take a few weeks; the conversation takes years.

Being well liked. Being liked is pleasant and it is not the same as being trusted. Some very likeable people cannot say a number without apologizing for it, and a parent reads that apology as a signal that the price is unjustified.

Sales experience, by itself. Depends entirely on what kind. Someone from a high-pressure environment can be actively harmful in a pediatric practice — the techniques that move a used car are the techniques that make a parent feel handled. Consultative sales, on the other hand, transfers well.

What actually predicts it

Comfort with silence. The single strongest predictor. The job contains repeated moments where the right move is to stop talking and let a parent think, and most people find that physically difficult.

Curiosity about the objection. Good coordinators are genuinely interested in why a parent is hesitating. Weak ones treat hesitation as an obstacle. The difference shows up in whether they ask a question or start a rebuttal.

Not personalizing a no. A coordinator who takes declined treatment as a verdict on themselves will start softening plans preemptively to avoid the feeling — and that is invisible, gradual, and expensive.

Being able to hold a position kindly. Can they say “I would not want to sedate her twice” to a parent pushing hard for something else, without either caving or getting rigid? This is rare and it is most of the job.

How to test for it in an interview

Stop asking what they would do. Everyone knows the right answer in the abstract. Make them do it.

Run a live roleplay. You be the parent. Give them a plan, a number, and one real objection. Then behave like an actual parent — hesitate, go quiet, say you need to think about it.

Watch for exactly three things:

  • Do they say the number cleanly, or hedge it?
  • When you go silent, how long before they fill it?
  • Do they ask you a question, or start listing payment options?

Fifteen minutes of this tells you more than an hour of interviewing. Most candidates fill the silence in under four seconds, and that is coachable — but you want to know before you hire, not after.

If you would rather not play the parent yourself, have the candidate run one Bella Coach rep against a hard persona. Watch for the same three things, and you have the scored rep to look at afterwards.

Tell the candidate what you are doing and why. “I am going to be a difficult parent for ten minutes — this is the actual job, and I would rather you experience it now.” The ones who find that interesting rather than alarming are usually the ones you want.

Treatment coordinator interview questions that reveal the real skill

One interview question earns its place above all the others:

“Tell me about a time you had to give someone news they did not want, and you could not fix it for them.”

You are listening for whether they stayed in the conversation or escaped it. People who escape describe how quickly they resolved it or handed it off. People who stay describe the other person's reaction, and often remember what was said.

That second group is who you want in a room with a parent who just heard a number they cannot afford. A few more questions test the same traits from different angles:

  1. “Say this out loud to me: ‘The total is three thousand four hundred dollars.’” Then wait. Listen for whether they say it cleanly or soften it, and whether they fill the pause that follows.
  2. “What would you say if a parent asked, ‘Is this really necessary?’” A good answer brings the doctor in. A worrying one argues the clinical case themselves (here is why).
  3. “Tell me about a no you took personally. What did you do differently next time?” You want someone who learned from it, not someone who started avoiding the situation.
  4. “A family says they need to think about it. What do you do in the next two minutes?” Strong candidates ask a question; weak ones list payment options or promise to call.
  5. “How would you explain an insurance estimate to a parent who has never seen one?” Listen for plain language and a dollar figure, not plan jargon.
  6. “What do you do when you disagree with how a plan was presented to you?” You are hiring someone who will hold a position kindly, with the doctor as well as with families.

After you hire

The standard onboarding is shadowing. Sit with an experienced coordinator, watch a dozen presentations, then take over.

The gap is that watching is not doing. The first time a new coordinator says a four-figure number out loud, it is to a real parent about a real child, and they will fumble it — not from ignorance, but because saying it is a physical skill they have never performed.

Give them the reps somewhere the stakes are not real. Roleplay with a colleague, on video, whatever you have. The goal is that their tenth time saying “three thousand four hundred dollars” happens before their first real presentation, rather than during their third week of them.
